Brand to Know: Après Golf — Ski Patches, Tie-Dye Fleece and a Rack of Dancing Bears

September 9, 2026 Drops & Brands 50 Pieces · $38 to $138
The TGI Take

What Après Golf is actually selling is provenance you cannot order twice. The fleece is hi-loft sherpa, hand-sewn in California, but the reason a cover costs $88 is the patch on the front — Chamonix, St. Moritz, Alta, Mad River Glen — cut off a ski jacket that someone wore somewhere before it got anywhere near a golf bag.

The Fruit Looper™ at $88 is the one to look at first, because it is the clearest version of the trick: a sherpa cover carrying a crest that has nothing to do with golf and somehow belongs on a bag anyway.

This suits the player who likes an object with a history attached and does not want the history to be his own brand’s logo. Fifty pieces are in stock across eight groups, and $38 is the cheapest way in.

The Story

Après Golf makes hi-loft sherpa fleece headcovers, hand-sewn in California, and sews vintage patches onto them — ski resorts, country clubs, and whatever else turns up. Fifty pieces are below, across eight groups, all of them in stock on 9 September 2026.

The idea starts with the name. Après-ski is the part of a ski day that happens after the skiing, and the patches that end up on these covers came off ski jackets: Chamonix, St. Moritz, Alta, Mad River Glen. Après buys them, cuts fleece around them, and sells the result to golfers. The brand describes what it does plainly on its own site — “We source vintage patches, create new patches, embroider logos and print custom designs.” The sewing happens in a California factory that has been running since 1992, at a mill that also supplies fleece to outdoor and fashion labels.

If you want the short version: the tie-dye prints are the ones people stop you about, the vintage patches are the ones that will not be repeatable, and the whole catalogue is really an advertisement for the custom programme, where you post them a patch of your own.

Where the Patches Come From

A supply chain that runs on ski swaps and clubhouse drawers rather than on a production calendar.

The archive is finite. Fifty-six club patches and eleven ski-resort patches are listed in stock today, and each design exists in whatever quantity Après managed to buy. There is no reorder. When a resort patch runs out it is gone in a way that a printed colourway never is, which is why the vintage rack turns over faster than the rest of the catalogue.

The ski patches are the older objects. Chamonix hosted the first Winter Olympics in 1924 and St. Moritz the second in 1928; Alta opened in 1938; Mad River Glen has been a skier-owned co-operative since 1995 and still refuses snowboards. Their patches were made to be sewn onto jackets and hats, in an era when a resort visit produced a physical souvenir rather than a geotag.

The club patches read differently. A Los Angeles Country Club or Olympic Club crest carries an access question with it — these are private clubs, and the patch is a thing a member or a guest once had. Après makes no claim about provenance and neither do we; what is on offer is the object, not the membership.

And the sweaters follow the same logic. Après describes the Sunday Sweater rail in its own product copy as “collecting vintage and vibrant golf sweaters from the ’80s and ’90s”, and ties it directly to the covers: each one has a story “just like the vintage patches we affix atop our American Made fleece headcovers.” One brand, one instinct, applied to two categories.

The Questions

What is a hi-loft sherpa fleece headcover?

Sherpa fleece is a knitted pile fabric with a deep, curled nap on the face. Hi-loft means the pile is left long rather than sheared flat, which is what gives an Après cover its bulk and why a printed colourway sinks into the surface instead of sitting on it. Après states its fleece comes from a mill that also supplies outdoor and fashion brands.

Where are Après Golf headcovers made?

Hand-sewn in California. Après states on its custom page that the factory doing the sewing has been in business since 1992. The brand markets the covers as American made.

Are the vintage patches real?

Après says it sources vintage patches, creates new patches, embroiders logos and prints custom designs, so the catalogue mixes all four. Individual listings do not carry provenance documentation, and Après makes no authenticity claim on a per-patch basis.

What is the reversible headcover for?

It carries a different patch on each face, so one cover serves two marks. Après describes it as patent pending and aims it at tournaments and corporate outings needing two logos, and at clubs running a member-only logo alongside a public one.

Can I put my own patch on one?

Yes. The Send Us Your Patch cover is $118 and works exactly as named — you post them the patch and they build the headcover around it. Après also runs a separate wholesale and custom programme through sales@apresgolf.com for pro shops, clubs and events.

What are the Sunday Sweaters?

Secondhand golf sweaters from the 1980s and 90s that Après buys in and resells, named after players rather than makers. They run $88 to $98 and each is a single garment in a single size, so the rail changes constantly.

How much do Après headcovers cost?

The range runs $38 to $138. Printed and solid fleece starts at $38, the Grateful-Dead-style bear covers sit at $88, and the vintage club and resort patches occupy the top of the range from $88 to $138. Shipping is free on all orders.
