Hancock Golf Course — The Oldest Course in Texas Is a $20 Nine in the Middle of Austin

August 10, 2026 Field Guide Est. 1899 · Penick’s First Course

Drive up 41st Street and you’ll miss it. There’s a shopping center on one side, Hyde Park bungalows on the other, and somewhere in between the buildings stop and there’s just… grass. Old oaks. A creek. Nine holes that have been sitting there since 1899.

That’s Hancock. It’s the oldest golf course in Texas, it costs twenty bucks, and it’s a few minutes north of downtown.

We love it for reasons that have almost nothing to do with golf. Cities are supposed to fill in their soft spots. That’s what cities do. Land in the middle of Austin costs what it costs now, and here is a chunk of it growing grass for people who want to walk around hitting a ball. It shouldn’t still be here. It is.

And it’s not precious about itself, which is the best part. There’s no pro shop. Nobody greets you. You walk up to a machine, tap a card, take your stub, and go — first come, first served, starting on the first tee like everyone else. No carts, no tee times, no starter telling you your group is late. Just a course, and you, and whoever else showed up.

The Course

How It Survived — 127 Years, Short Version

1899
Lewis Hancock raises money from Austin families to buy land and open a nine-hole course — the Austin Golf Club. Austin’s population is roughly 16,250.
1913
The course expands to 18 holes after the purchase of the Ernst Farm — 40 acres for a back nine.
1946
The City of Austin buys the property. The course is renamed for Lewis Hancock.
1951
The city takes over operations.
1962
Voters approve the sale of the back nine. It becomes the Hancock Shopping Center. Nine holes remain.
1980
Parks and Rec considers closing the course over an annual deficit. The Hancock Neighborhood Association fights to keep golf alive, and wins.
2008
Parks and Rec and the neighborhood association partner to build the walking trail that now rings the course, sharing maintenance.
2012
Reclaimed water and pay stations installed — the setup you play under today.
2014
The golf course is listed on the National Register of Historic Places. The recreation center is named a Recorded Texas Historic Landmark.

Know Before You Go

Rates
$20 regular, $16 sunset, $18 senior (60+), $18 college with student ID, $16 junior. Same price seven days a week — no weekend premium, which is rare in this town. The green fee is good for play all day.
Payment
Pay station only, credit and debit cards, no cash. Keep your stub — payment gets verified on the course.
Walking
No carts, no pull cart rental, no exceptions. Bring a stand bag or a push cart of your own.
Tee times
There aren’t any. First come, first served, and all play starts on the first hole.
Practice
A putting green, and that’s it. No range, no pro shop, no staff on site — questions go to 512-974-9350.
Closures
Closed one weekday a month for aerification (Sept. 16, Nov. 10 and Dec. 16 are next up) and Oct. 14 for overseeding. It reopens the following day.
Annual card
If you play here a lot, the GolfATX annual card drops Hancock to a $3.50 rate any day of the week — the math works out fast.

Why We Love It

Austin is not sentimental about its greenspace. In 1962 the city voted to sell Hancock’s back nine and it became a shopping center — nine holes of a 63-year-old golf course, traded for parking. In 1980 Parks and Rec looked at the deficit and floated closing what was left. The neighborhood showed up and said no, and that is the only reason there’s a course there today.

You can still feel that fight in the place. The walking trail that rings the course exists because Parks and Rec and the Hancock Neighborhood Association built it together in 2008 and agreed to share the upkeep. Golfers and dog walkers and strollers, all sharing the same patch of ground, mostly getting along. The Lions Muni fight has been the loud version of this argument for years. Hancock had its version quietly, decades earlier, and won.

So go play it. Not because it’s a great test of golf — it isn’t, and it doesn’t care. Go because you can leave work at six, walk nine holes under trees that were here before the highway, and be home for dinner having spent less than you would on two beers downtown. Go because the same ground taught Harvey Penick the game, and nobody charges extra for that.

A course this old, this cheap, this close in, still open to anyone who walks up with a debit card — that’s not nostalgia. That’s a city deciding, over and over for 127 years, to keep something instead of selling it. We’d like it to keep deciding that. The Hancock Golf Course Conservancy is working on exactly that, and the National Register listing in 2014 gave them something to point at.

The best thing you can do for it is show up and pay the twenty dollars.

Harvey Penick Grew Up Here

Everybody in Austin knows the name. Fewer people know he learned all of it on this nine.

Penick was born here in 1904, the youngest of five boys. In 1912, at eight years old, he started caddying at the Austin Country Club — which was, at the time, this golf course. He was an assistant pro at thirteen. Weekends, summers, after school. The club liked him enough to offer him the head professional job while he was still in high school, and he had to say no, because his family made him finish. So the club held the job open. He graduated Austin High in May 1923 and took it that month, at eighteen.

He stayed until 1950, when the club moved out to Riverside Drive. Thirty-eight years on this ground. Every bit of the foundation — the caddie years, the apprenticeship, the first two decades of teaching — happened on the holes you can play this afternoon for twenty dollars.

Somewhere in the 1930s, while he was working here, he started keeping notes in a red Scribbletex notebook, tabbed by subject: hooks, slices, putting. It was just a teaching aid. Sixty years later Bud Shrake talked him into publishing it and Harvey Penick’s Little Red Book became the best-selling sports book ever written.

He coached Texas from 1931 to 1963, mostly for free. His students included Ben Crenshaw, Tom Kite, Betsy Rawls, Mickey Wright, Kathy Whitworth, and Sandra Palmer — six of the thirteen women in the LPGA Hall of Fame came through him. Another was Morris Williams Jr., who has a muni of his own on the other side of town. Tommy Armour, the man most people called the best teacher alive, gave Penick the title instead. The PGA named him its first National Teacher of the Year in 1989. He died in Austin in 1995.

Eighty-two years with that club. Roughly half of them right here, on a course that now asks nothing of you but a debit card and comfortable shoes.

FAQ
What is the oldest golf course in Texas?
Hancock Golf Course in Austin, founded in 1899 by former mayor Lewis Hancock. It is still open to the public as a nine-hole muni.
How much does it cost to play Hancock?
$20 for a regular round, $16 at sunset, $18 for seniors 60 and older and for college students with ID, $16 for juniors. Same rates all seven days, and the fee covers play all day. Cards only at the pay station.
Does Hancock have carts?
No. Walking only, no cart rental and no pull carts. There’s no pro shop either — it’s a pay station operation, first come first served, all play starting on hole one.
What’s the Harvey Penick connection?
Penick began caddying here in 1912 at age eight, when the site was the original Austin Country Club. He became an assistant pro at thirteen and head professional in May 1923, weeks after graduating Austin High. He stayed at this location until the club moved to Riverside Drive in 1950 — 38 years on this ground — and his association with the club ran 82 years in total. The red notebook that became the Little Red Book was started here in the 1930s.
How long is the course?
Nine holes, par 35, playing up and down hills and across Waller Creek. Short by modern standards with small period greens. It had 18 holes and sand greens once.
Is the course historically protected?
It was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 2014, and the recreation center next door is a Recorded Texas Historic Landmark. The Hancock Golf Course Conservancy advocates for the greenspace.
