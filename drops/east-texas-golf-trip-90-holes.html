90 Holes in Three Days: Tempest, Wild Spring Dunes, LumberJack National and Pine Dunes

Field Note East Texas · played November 6–8, 2026
Saturday morning at Wild Spring Dunes. Photo: The Grassy Issue

“For the weekend it was our golf mecca.”— Lenny

Four of us went: me, Jeff, Matt and Todd, the guys coming in from all over. Ninety holes in three days across four courses: Tempest twice on Friday, Wild Spring Dunes and LumberJack National on Saturday, Pine Dunes on Sunday.

The drive out was very boring. Then you get into the Pines and the ecology becomes a lot more interesting. It’s wild how much, and how quickly, Texas can change ecosystems.

East Texas is completely out of the way for most people. It’s isolated, and we didn’t find a single great restaurant. But there was variety, there were deeply challenging holes, and the best part of the golf was simple: we did not have to wait on a tee box once all weekend.

Day I

Friday

36 holes · Tempest, twice

The drive out was the dull part. Once the road gets into the Pines, the country changes fast: pines close in on both sides and the ground starts to roll. Friday was all Tempest. The four of us rode two loops under a heavy, shifting sky, straight stroke play in the morning and the pairs match in the afternoon. By the end of the second loop the light was going, with the big flag over the 9th green still flying.

01

Tempest Golf Club

Gladewater, Texas · Jeff Brauer redesign · reopened 2018

Friday was Tempest, twice. The club sits outside Gladewater and is Jeff Brauer’s complete redesign of the old Southern Hills course, reopened in 2018. It isn’t a private club: anyone can book a tee time, and the Dallas Morning News has rated it the 16th-best public course in Texas.

The land has been through a few lives. The Webb family opened a nine-hole course called Shallow Creek here in 1989, with a double-wide trailer for a clubhouse. It grew to 18 holes as Southern Hills in 1996, then struggled. New Orleans attorney Joe Bruno bought it in 2006 and brought in Brauer for a total renovation in 2016. What was meant to take eight months took more than two years, and the course reopened in the summer of 2018 with a dozen new holes. It has since hosted a local U.S. Amateur qualifier. The giant American flag you see from half the course went up on Veterans Day 2020 and stands over the 9th green.

From the black tees it runs 7,229 yards, par 72, rated 73.7 with a 130 slope, and there are five sets of tees down to 4,512. There are four par 5s, two on each nine, and the front side finishes with back-to-back long ones at the 6th and 9th, both over 560 from the tips. The rankings have followed: the Dallas Morning News has it 68th among the top 100 courses in Texas and 16th among those open to the public in 2026, Golf Digest named it the 18th-best course you can play in Texas in 2022, and Texas Monthly put it third on its 2023 list of public courses.

What got me first was the elevation. The course climbs and drops through the pines more than you expect from East Texas, the layout is tight, and it was as well manicured as anything we played all weekend. We rode both loops.

Round one was straight play. Round two was match play: the top player from the morning partnered the bottom player, and the middle two played together. The scores were tight and competitive all weekend.

The front nine is the more interesting side, with some great risk/reward holes. Playing the course twice in one day gave us a real sense of where to go for it off the tee.

Day II

Saturday

36 holes · Wild Spring Dunes, then LumberJack National

Saturday was the big one. We had one of the first tee times at Wild Spring Dunes and stood on the first tee with the sun barely over the pines, all four of us, push carts lined up because the course is walking only. Dexter forecaddied us around. After the round Jeff and Todd peeled off for home, and Matt and I drove south to Nacogdoches for a second 18 at LumberJack National, a different kind of golf entirely, played fast and finished in the long shadows of late afternoon.

02

Wild Spring Dunes

Mount Enterprise, Texas · Tom Doak · opened 2026

Saturday started with one of the first tee times at Wild Spring Dunes, the Tom Doak course outside Mount Enterprise that opened all 18 holes on September 8. We wrote about it before it opened in On Our Radar. Now we’ve played it.

The short version of how it happened: in 2021 a Dallas office worker named Brett Messerall, who had taught himself to read topo maps and soil surveys, found the land while a timber company was clear-cutting it. Underneath the pines was sand. He cold-emailed Tom Doak, who walked the site and later told D CEO, “This is a really good site.” Michael Keiser Jr., whose family built Bandon Dunes and Sand Valley, came in as developer. It sits on 2,400 acres, and a second course by Coore & Crenshaw is already being built.

The course plays in two counterclockwise loops. The front eight ramble over gentler hills, then the 9th sends you back off the starting hilltop into heavier ground and along deeper ravines. The fairways are zoysia, and eleven bridges tie the property together. Two holes get the most attention: the 10th, a long par 3 that reaches past 240 yards from the tips to a green perched above bunkers and a ravine, and the 17th, a 331-yard par 4 back across the same ravine to a skinny two-level green. There is no hotel yet, and the clubhouse is a temporary one called The Hitching Post. Keiser says the property could one day hold as many as five 18-hole courses.

Very, very nice. They threw an incredible amount of money at this course, and it shows. It is dynamic and stunning, and the greens were extremely fast. Some of the pin locations were wildly difficult: an inch or two past the hole and the ball rolled off the green.

We had a forecaddie, Dexter, who gave us good intel all morning, though he had a bias toward overreading putts. The marshal was a little pushy about pace of play, which might have been because members were playing right behind us.

The merch was solid, but it isn’t taking any risks.

03

LumberJack National

Nacogdoches, Texas · Formerly Piney Woods Country Club

Jeff and Todd headed home after Wild Spring, and Matt and I drove over to LumberJack National in Nacogdoches for the afternoon. It is a very casual track with a muni feel, the course that used to be Piney Woods Country Club, and it could not be more different from the morning.

LumberJack dates to 1926, which makes it the oldest course on this trip by a long way. It sits off Highway 59 on the south side of Nacogdoches, which bills itself as the oldest town in Texas, and the club calls itself the “Hidden Gem of East Texas” for its towering pines and big oaks. The fairways are narrow and the greens are small and quick, recently renovated, so position matters more than distance. It plays par 72 at about 6,190 yards from the back tees, short by modern standards, but the hills and trees make up for it. Today it runs as a golf course and event venue, with a pro shop, a pool, a bar and a restaurant, and tee times open five days ahead with the cart included in the rate.

It’s hilly and tree-lined, and very booby-trapped for wild hogs. We were pretty dialed in by then. We played very well, and fast.

Day III

Sunday

18 holes · Pine Dunes

Sunday came in grey. Matt and I drove to Frankston for the last round of the trip, the fifth in three days, at Pine Dunes. The starter met us at the first tee in his old Buick. By then the scoring was generous and we were happy to swing hard off every tee. Then the long drive home.

04

Pine Dunes Resort & Golf Club

Frankston, Texas · Jay Morrish and Carter Morrish · 2001

Sunday, Matt and I finished at Pine Dunes in Frankston, a Jay Morrish design built with his son Carter, opened in 2001. It’s a tight course, and the starter pulled his old Buick right up to the first tee box, which is awesome.

The story behind it is a good one. Jodi Lutz, who had never played golf, found the property in 1994 as a nine-hole course with an 88-acre lake; the pines reminded her of growing up in northern Minnesota. A few years later PGA Tour player David Frost walked the land, saw what it could be and introduced her to Jay Morrish. Morrish and his son Carter started work in late 1999, and it opened in 2001. The club’s own pitch is that there are no trick holes, no forced carries and water rarely comes into play.

The praise piled up fast. Golf Digest put it sixth on its list of America’s best new upscale public courses in 2002 and gives it 4.5 stars, Golfweek ranked it the No. 1 course you can play in Texas from 2010 to 2012 and again from 2014 to 2018, and the Golf Channel called it America’s No. 1 hidden gem. From the gold tees it stretches to 7,117 yards (74.4/131). Rates are $99 Monday to Thursday and $149 Friday to Sunday with cart and range balls included, replays are half price Sunday to Thursday, and there are guest condos on site that sleep a group of up to 80.

We lost a good bit of balls. By round five we were happy to take bigger risks off the tee, and unless it was a par or a birdie we were pretty generous with our scores.

Off the Course

Dinner

We got Costco lasagna and big ribeyes and grilled at the Airbnb.

The format

Friday’s second loop was match play: top player with the bottom player, the middle two together. The scores were tight and competitive all weekend.

The pace

We never waited on a tee box. Not once, at any of the four courses, across all 90 holes.

The scoring

By Sunday we were happy to take bigger risks off the tee, and unless it was a par or a birdie we were pretty generous with the card.

The verdict

East Texas is absolutely coming into its own as a golf destination. It’s out of the way and there is nowhere great to eat, but for one weekend it was our golf mecca: Tempest’s elevation, Wild Spring’s greens, a fast casual round at LumberJack and a starter’s Buick at Pine Dunes.

As for my own game, it has never felt further from where I want it. I had some truly great drives and approach shots, and I made some long putts. But putting it all together for 450-plus shots over three days makes me feel like we still have a ways to go.
