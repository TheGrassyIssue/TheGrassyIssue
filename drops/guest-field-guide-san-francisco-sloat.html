Guest Field Guide No. 01 · San Francisco · with SLOAT

San Francisco, According to SLOAT

By Lenny Harrington, Founder & Editor · Updated November 25, 2026

“Some of the best and most varied golf in the country, with legendary private clubs, incredible public courses, and weather that can humble anyone.”

Guides: Dan Fibiger & Teague Moriarty5 courses · 11 spots · 1 interview
The Idea

We handed our Field Guide to the people who make the clothes. Each Guest Field Guide is a city told by the founders of an independent golf brand: the courses they play, where they eat after, what they wear, and an interview about the game. They write it; we edit, check the facts and lay it out. No one paid for this.

No. 01 is San Francisco, by SLOAT. SLOAT is named for Sloat Boulevard, which runs west past some of the city’s best courses and ends at Ocean Beach. Dan Fibiger and Teague Moriarty founded it in 2026 with one rule: no synthetic fabrics. Both of them surf, and both of them play. Their words below are their own, trimmed for length.

Your Guides

Dan Fibiger & Teague Moriarty, SLOAT

“We’re the founders of SLOAT, a San Francisco-based golf apparel brand endeavoring to make the future heirlooms of golf. Clothes you’ll want to keep forever.”

Dan spent nearly 20 years in the human rights and environmental side of the apparel industry. Teague spent much of his career as a chef and is an owner of Sons & Daughters and Kiln, two Michelin-starred restaurants in San Francisco. @sloat.cc · sloat.cc

Where We Play

Three Rounds We Play All the Time

Every week. Two public courses in the city and one just south of it. Notes in mono are ours, checked November 25, 2026.

01

Crystal Springs Golf Course

Burlingame · public · 18 holes

Why we play itIt’s a challenging track with a great community, particularly through the men’s club.

The hole we loveNine. It’s one of the more challenging holes, with a really narrow window for your drive and an approach into a very tiered green. You have to know where to land it depending on the pin position. It’s not an easy hole, but getting a par or birdie on it is very satisfying.

One tipDon’t try to smash driver. You can often hit it 200 yards and get around the dogleg. It’s safer, with red stakes right and OB left, and it’s easy to lose a ball.

Public, 18 holes, south of the city off I-280. crystalspringsgolf.com
02

TPC Harding Park

Lake Merced, San Francisco · city-owned · 18 holes

Why we play itIt’s ten minutes from my house, it’s beautiful, and you can spray your driver around without losing your ball most of the time. It’s also hosted a PGA Championship, so you’re playing a course with some serious history.

The hole we loveThirteen and fourteen. They’re both fun par fours where you have to know where to place your drive to have a good approach shot in.

One tipCheck how thick the rough is. If it’s recently cut, being off the fairway isn’t very punishing. When they let it grow out, it can be really challenging and cost you distance.

Owned by the City of San Francisco, beside Lake Merced; it hosted the 2020 PGA Championship. The nine-hole Fleming course sits next door. tpc.com/harding-park
03

Presidio Golf Course

The Presidio, San Francisco · public · 18 holes

Why we play itI’ve always loved the Presidio in general — the landscape and nature, and how it feels like this hidden place within San Francisco, so far removed from the big city. The golf course really carries that same feeling through.

The hole we loveEleven. It’s a very hard par four, and you really have to hit a good drive to have an approach. Otherwise, you’re looking at 180 or 200 yards in.

One tipCarry it over the right edge of the bunkers on the left side of the fairway. If you hit a good drive, you’ll get a good roll down to the bottom of the hill.

Public, 18 holes, inside the Presidio. SLOAT holds a monthly round here. presidiogolf.com
Where We Play

Two Rounds We Travel For

Special days. The rounds they save up for, both a flight away.

04

PGA West, Pete Dye Mountain Course

La Quinta, near Palm Springs · resort · 18 holes

What makes it specialI love getting out of the cold Northern California weather and going down to Palm Springs in the winter. It’s such a beautiful course, especially on the back nine when you get up into the little canyons and valleys.

Insider knowledgeThe greens are raised and slope away, so they’re always very fast. Play the distance to the front of the green, not the middle or back, or you’ll roll off the back a lot.

Pete Dye designed it; it opened in 1981 and sits against the Santa Rosa Mountains. Resort course; book through PGA West. pgawest.com
05

Waiehu Municipal Golf Course

Wailuku, Maui · county muni · 18 holes

What makes it specialWhen I go to Maui, I’m mostly there to surf, but I golf a little too. I’ve played some of the nicer bucket-list courses there, which were great, but I kept coming back to Waiehu. It’s super affordable, fun, not very busy, and right against the ocean.

Insider knowledgeDon’t go left into the ocean.

Run by Maui County, on the coast just north of Wailuku. Residents pay less than visitors; check the county’s current rates before you go.

In Your Words

The Interview

Answers from Dan and Teague. Where one founder is speaking, we say so.

Just for SLOAT

SLOAT is named for Sloat Boulevard, which runs past the city’s courses to Ocean Beach. What does a golf day along it look like?

Within a two- or three-mile radius of Sloat Boulevard, you can play at some of the most exclusive private clubs in the country or some of the best public courses in California. The experiences couldn’t be more different, but the through line is the climate.

You can encounter wind, fog, warmth, cold, wet, and dry conditions, sometimes within the same 18 holes. The clubs themselves are incredibly varied, but the weather is every bit as dynamic. That’s part of what makes golf in this little corner of San Francisco so interesting.

You both surf and play. On a good morning, how do you fit in both, and which one wins when you can’t? · Dan

That’s the holy grail, isn’t it? Ideally, you tee off at 6 or 6:30 in the morning, squeeze in a three-and-a-half-hour round, and still make it to Ocean Beach before the wind turns on and blows out the surf.

As for which one wins when you can’t do both, I think Teague and I might have different answers. For me, surfing still wins ten times out of ten. I’ll let Teague speak for himself.

Your founders’ letter says most golf apparel is built around a version of the game neither of you found interesting. What’s the version you do find interesting?

The version of golf I find interesting is less about performance and more about the social side of the game. I’ve always thought of golf a bit like running marathons. As an amateur, I’m never going to win the race, but I can always try to do better than I did previously.

A lot of what appeals to me is the camaraderie with other players, getting out in nature, the rules and history of the game, and having this framework to meet people you might never have met otherwise and have a great time.

How do the fog and wind out in the Outerlands change a round, and how did it shape what you make?

First, we wanted to make clothes that actually work where we play golf. Second, we wanted them to have an aesthetic that didn’t necessarily announce to the world that you’d just been golfing.

The fog and wind out here are very real. Anyone who’s played enough golf in San Francisco has experienced a round where visibility drops to 30 or 40 feet, or where you’re playing in 30-mile-an-hour winds.

We wanted to make clothes that could handle all of that without looking like conventional technical golf apparel.

You launched with a party at Golden Gate Park Golf Course. Why there?

It’s arguably one of the best nine-hole courses in the country. It was renovated a couple of years ago and is now an incredibly fun little track.

It sits at the western edge of Golden Gate Park, practically on Ocean Beach, and the people who work there are awesome. Given that it’s just down the street from Sloat Boulevard, it felt like the perfect symbol of the brand and what we’re trying to build.

Teague, what has running a kitchen taught you that shows up in how you make clothes, or how you play?

The biggest thing that’s carried over from running kitchens to SLOAT and golf is how foundational systems are. You don’t start out operating at a two- or three-star restaurant level, or as a scratch golfer. You get there over time by coming in every day and trying to be a little better than you were the day before. Those small gains in quality and skill add up if you keep at it.

Dan, after nearly 20 years in apparel, what did you decide SLOAT would never do?

I’ve always thought it would be interesting to build a brand within a very specific set of constraints. Constraints force focus and discipline, but I also think they can be an incredible engine for innovation.

For us, the hard-and-fast rule is that we won’t use synthetic fabrics. At the same time, we want to make extraordinarily high-performing apparel. Reconciling those two ambitions gives us a pretty interesting mandate to work within, and I think it ultimately pushes us to make better things.

Golden Gate Park, Lincoln Park, Sharp Park, the Presidio: which one do you send a visiting friend to first?

Probably the Presidio. Visiting friends usually want to play 18, and it’s an awesome course with some special San Francisco scenery. All four are great and offer very different experiences, but I find myself recommending the Presidio most often.

The game and you

How did you get hooked on golf?

I grew up near a golf course, and from about age seven I spent almost every summer day there. I’d take the bus over in the morning, hit balls, play nine holes, and eventually work my way up to playing 36 holes a day.

The best part was the friendships. At seven or eight, I was hanging out with kids who were 11 or 12, which felt like the coolest thing in the world. We spent entire summers playing golf, drinking Coke, eating French fries, and generally having the time of our lives. That’s what got me hooked.

How many times have you quit golf, and what pulled you back? · Dan

Just once. I stopped playing when I was about 16 or 17, partly because I was starting to suspect that being a really good golfer wasn’t particularly impressive to girls. I also skateboarded and snowboarded, which seemed like a better use of my time from a social standpoint.

I didn’t pick up the game again until 2025, when Teague dragged me back out. So I took a very legitimate 27-year break. Turns out I still love it.

What’s the best shot you’ve ever hit, and did anyone see it?

The one that sticks with me came at the Old Course at Half Moon Bay, after I got back into golf. I was playing a match against my brother, who was four strokes up on me.

We were on the 15th, a long par five with water running down the left side and the wind in our faces. I was about 250 yards into the green and pulled out my three-wood. My natural shot is a fade, so I aimed out over the water, flushed it, and watched it fade back onto the green to about 15 feet.

I missed the eagle putt but made a birdie. My brother made a triple, and suddenly the match was even. I ended up losing the match, but whatever.

What golf superstition or habit can’t you shake? · Dan

I think the problem with quitting golf at 16 and picking it back up at 43 is that my approach to course management is still firmly rooted in the mind of a 16-year-old.

Given a choice between logic, common sense and sound course management on one hand and the chance of pulling off something heroic on the other, I’ll choose the hero shot every time. It fails 99 times out of 100.

What’s the round you still tell people about?

The first time I broke 80. I shot a 79, but I had two eagles, three birdies, a bunch of doubles, and a couple of triples. I always joke that the other times I’ve broken 80, it’s been by making a bunch of pars and bogeys. That feels like a much more sustainable way to do it than relying on eagles and birdies.

What’s your most embarrassing moment on a course?

Anytime I’m playing with Teague, I’m generally embarrassed by my association with him.

Your people

Who’s in your regular group?

It’s a mix of friends from different parts of my life. Any friend of mine who plays golf, I’ll play with. And through golf, you inevitably end up making new friends. It’s just this great hodgepodge of guys who all happen to love this weird little game.

Who made you feel like you belonged in golf?

When I was a kid, it was the older boys I played with. When you’re 11 and hanging around with 13- or 14-year-olds, they’re definitely going to make fun of you, but there’s also this big-brother dynamic where they take you under their wing.

There’s a camaraderie to the game that makes you feel like you’re part of something, even when you’re getting relentlessly teased.

What has golf taught you that works off the course too?

Nobody cares. And I mean that in the best possible way. Nobody cares if you shot 73. Nobody cares if you shot 103. Straight up, nobody cares.

Just do you, enjoy yourself, and stop worrying so much about what everyone else thinks.

What would make someone who’s never played feel at home on your course?

Follow the basic rules, be chill, and don’t be a jerk. That’s pretty much it.

Your style

Whose golf style do you borrow from?

I don’t really look to golf for style inspiration. We’re much more interested in clothes that work on the golf course but don’t necessarily look like golf clothes.

Ideally, you can walk off the 18th green and go about the rest of your day without looking like you just played 18 holes.

What’s the worst outfit you’ve ever worn on a course?

When I came back to golf in 2025, I needed some golf clothes, so I went into a local pro shop and did the best I could with what was available.

I came home with a full polyester performance outfit, and I remember showing my wife, who looked at me with a combination of shock and horror. She wanted to know what had happened to me. It was one of the early arguments for starting SLOAT.

What’s the first piece of golf gear you bought with your own money?

I saved up for a Scotty Cameron putter when I was about 15. My dad agreed to pay for half, and I covered the rest. At the time, it was the coolest thing I’d ever owned.

Besides clubs, what three things do you never play without?

An apple, trail mix, and a bottle of water.

Your brand

When did a stranger first ask about something you made?

We had some graphic hoodies made early on, and our friend Lee, who designed our logo, absolutely smashed it. We started getting a lot of people coming up and asking what SLOAT was.

It was pretty cool to see people take an interest in something we’d created before they even knew what it was.

How does an idea turn into a finished piece?

It starts with a simple question: what problem are we trying to solve? From there, we think about what the product should be, what it might look like, and how it needs to function.

Then comes the technical design, sourcing fabrics, working with mills and factories, developing colors, reviewing lab dips, and going through multiple rounds of sampling and revisions until we feel like we’ve nailed the fit, proportions, and details.

The funny thing is, you can start with an idea as simple as “let’s make a vest,” but between that initial thought and the finished garment, there are probably a thousand little decisions to make.

What’s the piece you’re proudest of?

I’m most stoked on our use of Ventile cotton. It’s this extraordinary fabric that, for whatever reason, fell out of favor over the years, and we think that’s a real shame. Our Ventile outerwear is the best expression of what we’re trying to do with SLOAT.

We want these pieces to be treated as heirlooms. If someone buys one of our jackets, vests, or wind shirts, we want them to hold on to it forever.

And as romantic as it sounds, I love the idea that one day some kid might inherit his dad’s old golf jacket. That it becomes something he treasures, not just because it’s a great piece of clothing, but because it reminds him of his dad and of a relationship forged through golf.

How do you want someone to feel when they put on something you made?

We want them to feel like they got incredible value for their money, that they look really, really good, and that they’re about to go out and shoot a personal best.

What’s one thing you’d like to try making?

Shoes. We’ve already spent a lot of time on footwear, about as much as on some of the clothes we’ve brought to market.

We’ve got some pretty incredible prototypes sitting in our office, but we put the project on hold for a few reasons. I stare at those samples almost every day, wondering when we’ll have the time and capacity to pick the project back up.

What do you hope golf looks like in ten years?

I hope it’s even more diverse than it is today, in every sense of the word. At the same time, I hope there’s a renewed appreciation for the traditions and etiquette of the game.

Most importantly, I hope we return to a world where every sprinkler head has a yardage marker. Frankly, I think there should be a federal law mandating it.

“I love the idea that one day some kid might inherit his dad’s old golf jacket.”— SLOAT, on its Ventile outerwear
